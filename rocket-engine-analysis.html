Rocket Engine Analysis

Connecting thermodynamic cycles to propulsion performance.

A paper coauthored with Chris West examining how pressure ratio, temperature limits, and exhaust momentum shape engine performance. The analysis develops ideal Brayton-cycle relationships, then considers the engineering losses that separate an ideal model from a working engine.

Project overview

From heat input to useful output.

How do the conditions inside an engine determine its efficiency, thrust, and power?

The paper connects component-level descriptions to a thermodynamic model: compression, heat addition, expansion, and heat rejection. It uses ideal-gas and isentropic relationships to derive thermal efficiency, a momentum balance to describe thrust, and differentiation to identify the operating point for maximum specific work.

This is an analytical literature study. Its outcomes are derivations and a synthesis of engineering considerations; it does not report a built engine or experimental performance measurements.

Model scope

Distinguishing the engine architectures.

The original paper is titled Rocket Engine Analysis and discusses both rocket hardware and air-breathing turbine engines. Its compressor–combustor–turbine cycle analysis describes an ideal Brayton gas-turbine model.

A rocket carries its oxidizer onboard, so its thrust balance has no atmospheric inlet-momentum term. The cycle relationships below are labeled for the Brayton model; the rocket thrust relationship is shown separately to make that distinction explicit.

Technical analysis

Three performance questions.

The central relationships connect an ideal cycle's pressure ratio, exhaust momentum, and temperature limits to different measures of performance.

01 · Ideal Brayton cycle

What sets thermal efficiency?

ηth=1−rp−γ−1γ

For constant specific heats and ideal isentropic compression and expansion, thermal efficiency increases with pressure ratio rp = p2/p1. Here γ is the ratio of specific heats. This is cycle thermal efficiency, distinct from overall propulsive efficiency.

02 · Rocket momentum balance

What produces thrust?

F=m˙Ve+(pe−pa)Ae

Rocket thrust combines exhaust momentum with a pressure contribution at the nozzle exit. The paper develops the broader air-breathing balance, which also subtracts incoming air momentum. Pressure thrust vanishes only when exit and ambient pressures match.

03 · Maximum Brayton specific work

Where is work output largest?

T2,opt=T1T3

With inlet temperature T1 and maximum cycle temperature T3 fixed, differentiating net specific work gives this optimum compressor exit temperature. The result assumes an ideal cycle with constant specific heats.

Central takeaway

Maximum efficiency and maximum work occur at different operating points.

A higher pressure ratio improves ideal Brayton thermal efficiency, but does not indefinitely increase net work.

At fixed inlet and peak temperatures, increasing compression raises the compressor exit temperature and reduces the heat that can be added. As that temperature approaches the cycle maximum, both heat input and net work approach zero.

The maximum-work condition balances compressor demand against turbine output. For a fixed mass flow rate, maximizing specific work also maximizes ideal net shaft power.

Beyond the ideal model

Engineering the losses.

  • Irreversibility: real compression and expansion produce entropy and reduce useful work.
  • Thermal limits: material temperatures constrain the maximum cycle temperature, while heat transfer affects the energy balance.
  • Cycle modifications: the paper discusses intercooling, regeneration, and reheating. Their efficiency effects depend on the full cycle configuration.
  • Propellant delivery: rocket turbopumps supply fuel and oxidizer at the pressures required by the combustion chamber.
  • Nozzle expansion: exhaust velocity and exit pressure both contribute to thrust, making ambient conditions part of nozzle performance.

Building a thermodynamic argument.

The work connects physics, mathematical derivation, and engineering interpretation.

It draws together ideal-gas relations, energy and momentum balances, optimization by differentiation, and technical literature. Coauthoring the paper also required organizing those ideas into a shared explanation of propulsion performance.
