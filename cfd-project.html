Computational Fluid Dynamics Solver Suite

From numerical methods to the two-dimensional compressible Navier–Stokes equations.

A modular CFD environment developed independently while working through John D. Anderson Jr.’s Computational Fluid Dynamics: The Basics with Applications. It advances from foundational discretization to nozzle flow, supersonic expansion, viscous Couette flow, and a complete compressible-flow solver.

Why I built it

Learning CFD by building the machinery.

My physics studies—especially senior work in supersonic compressible inviscid flow—led me toward fluid dynamics, high-speed aerodynamics, propulsion, and simulation.

I wanted to move beyond analytical solutions and understand how governing equations become reliable numerical software. This project became a self-directed study in translating physical models into discrete algorithms, validating them, and organizing them into reusable engineering tools.

What I learned

Connecting physics, numerics, and software.

  • Translate PDEs and boundary conditions into computational algorithms.
  • Connect physical behavior with truncation error, stability, and convergence.
  • Separate reusable numerical infrastructure from model-specific physics.
  • Validate with exact solutions where available—and use honest diagnostic hierarchies where they are not.
  • Trace the progression from model equations and Euler flow to complete Navier–Stokes.

Solver progression

Complexity, built in layers.

Each stage adds new physics or numerical demands; earlier tools become infrastructure for the application solvers that follow.

  1. 01

    Numerical foundations

    Model PDEs & discrete operators

    Finite differences, interpolation, RK4, linear algebra, CFL control, error norms, diffusion, and elliptic relaxation.

  2. 02

    Transport

    Linear advection

    Periodic wave transport with method of lines, Lax–Wendroff, and MacCormack; checked against the analytical translation.

  3. 03

    Chapter 7

    Quasi-1-D nozzle flow

    Euler equations in primitive and conservative form, choked and subsonic flow, then a captured normal shock with artificial viscosity.

  4. 04

    Chapter 8

    Prandtl–Meyer expansion

    Steady 2-D Euler equations marched in space on a boundary-fitted grid, including flux transforms and Abbett wall correction.

  5. 05

    Chapter 9

    Viscous Couette flow

    Momentum diffusion with implicit Crank–Nicolson, plus pressure correction and SIMPLE on a staggered grid.

  6. 06

    Chapter 10

    Compressible Navier–Stokes

    Laminar Mach-4 flat-plate flow combining compressibility, viscous stresses, heat conduction, and explicit MacCormack marching.

Software engineering

Modular CFD architecture.

Models define what is solved. Solvers advance the state. Numerical kernels perform one discrete operation.

Application-specific physics remains separate from reusable grids, numerical operators, linear algebra, plotting, and infrastructure.

+cfd
├── +grids       grid construction
├── +linalg      tridiagonal systems
├── +models      equations & exact solutions
├── +numerics    discrete operators
├── +physics     transport properties
├── +plotting    scientific visualization
├── +solvers

Analytical

Known solutions

Advection translation, diffusion eigenmodes, harmonic Laplace fields, isentropic area–Mach states, expansion relations, and the linear Couette profile.

Reference

Textbook behavior

Anderson station values, downstream states, normal-shock relations, and documented application-case behavior.

Diagnostics

Physical checks

Positivity, conservation, boundaries, residuals, wall tangency, mass flow, boundary-layer trends, and grid sensitivity.

Transparent limitation: the arbitrary-field Chapter 9 SIMPLE example remains finite but does not reach its requested convergence tolerance within the default iteration limit. The complete Navier–Stokes case has no exact solution, so it is assessed with unit and manufactured-gradient tests, physical behavior, conservation, and grid sensitivity.

The culmination

The complete compressible Navier–Stokes solver.

Chapter 10 brings the project’s earlier layers together: conservation of mass, momentum and energy; compressibility; viscous stresses; temperature-dependent viscosity; heat conduction; application boundaries; stability control; and explicit time marching.

Everything built earlier becomes infrastructure for a two-dimensional, laminar Mach-4 flat-plate calculation.

Self-directed study

Theory → implementation → testing → validation → visualization.

John D. Anderson Jr.’s Computational Fluid Dynamics: The Basics with Applications is the principal educational reference. The suite is an independent implementation created while studying and validating against that presentation; it does not claim the underlying algorithms as original inventions.
