“Beyond the Sound Barrier” - An Analysis of Supersonic Air Flow

An analytical study of the physics that governs flight beyond the sound barrier.

This capstone developed a simplified analytical treatment of supersonic, compressible, inviscid flow from conservation laws derived with the Reynolds Transport Theorem. The framework was applied to normal and oblique shocks, ramjet heat addition, and piezoelectric pressure measurement before considering where perfect-gas and inviscid assumptions break down in hypersonic flight.

Paper abstract

A framework for understanding supersonic flight.

To revolutionize the aviation industry, supersonics must be studied to improve the efficiency of supersonic aircraft such as the Concorde. Supersonics, however, occupy a distinct physical regime in which compressibility, thermodynamics, and wave phenomena fundamentally alter fluid behavior. This paper develops a simplified treatment of supersonic compressible inviscid flow relevant to supersonic vehicles, beginning with the applied conservation laws derived from the Reynolds Transport Theorem. This treatment is applied to study one- and two-dimensional wave phenomena such as shocks and regions of heat addition. Finally, a pressure measurement technique is discussed to highlight how these findings are used in practice. Together, these topics provide a coherent introduction to the physics governing supersonic flight.

Project overview

From fundamental physics to high-speed flight.

The project investigated the physical framework governing supersonic compressible flow and connected that framework to flow over and through high-speed aerospace vehicles.

Starting from conservation of mass, momentum, and energy, the analysis uses the Reynolds Transport Theorem to derive the Euler equations for compressible inviscid flow. The work used simplified one- and two-dimensional analytical models of a calorically perfect, non-reacting gas.

Aerospace relevance

Why compressibility matters.

  • Shock waves create abrupt changes in pressure, temperature, density, and velocity.
  • Propulsion performance depends on controlling compression and heat addition.
  • Measurements must account for strong flow disturbances and high temperatures.
  • Hypersonic conditions expose the limits of ideal-gas and inviscid assumptions.

Research areas

The technical scope.

Ten connected topics build from governing principles to propulsion, measurement, and the physical limits encountered at higher Mach numbers.

01

Compressible Flow Fundamentals

Examined how density variations couple pressure, temperature, and velocity in high-speed gas flows.

02

Mach Number and Flow Regimes

Used the ratio of flow speed to local sound speed to distinguish subsonic, transonic, supersonic, and hypersonic behavior.

03

Conservation Laws / Reynolds Transport Theorem

Derived control-volume forms of mass, momentum, and energy conservation as the foundation of compressible-flow analysis.

04

Normal Shock Waves

Studied one-dimensional discontinuities that decelerate supersonic flow while raising static pressure, temperature, and entropy.

05

Oblique Shock Waves

Analyzed shocks produced when supersonic flow turns into itself, a central feature of wedges, inlets, and lifting surfaces.

06

Supersonic Pressure Measurement

Connected shock-induced mechanical stress to measurable electrical signals in piezoelectric pressure transducers.

07

Heat Addition and Ramjet Propulsion

Modeled one-dimensional combustor heat addition through changes in stagnation temperature and downstream Mach number.

08

Aerodynamic Heating

Examined why thin shock layers and large temperature gradients make heating central to hypersonic vehicle analysis.

09

Piezoelectric Instrumentation

Used polarization, electric displacement, and the piezoelectric tensor to explain pressure-sensor transduction.

10

Hypersonic Flight Considerations

Identified where calorically perfect, non-reacting, inviscid models fail as viscosity, reactions, dissociation, and ionization emerge.

Key physics

A few relationships organize the flow.

The capstone used compact analytical models to connect local flow state, conservation principles, and shock-induced property changes.

Flow regime

Mach number

M=Va

Mach number measures flow speed relative to the local propagation speed of pressure disturbances.

Acoustic speed

Speed of sound

a=γRT

For a calorically perfect gas, sound speed rises with temperature and sets the local scale for compressibility.

Research process

Learning an engineering discipline from first principles.

The capstone centered on reviewing academic and technical literature, interpreting compressible-flow theory, and synthesizing thermodynamics, fluid mechanics, wave physics, and electromagnetism-based instrumentation into one coherent account of high-speed aerospace systems.

  1. 01

    Research

    Reviewed literature in compressible flow, aerodynamics, thermal physics, scramjet propulsion, piezoelectric sensors, and electromagnetism.

  2. 02

    Derive

    Worked from Reynolds Transport Theorem to integral conservation equations for mass, momentum, and energy.

  3. 03

    Synthesize

    Applied the theory to one-dimensional normal shocks, two-dimensional oblique shocks, and ramjet heat addition.

  4. 04

    Communicate

    Built a 40-page technical narrative connecting advanced physics to aerospace applications and measurement.
